Bathroom renovation approvals in Newcastle

In short: A bathroom renovation that keeps the room layout is often exempt development in NSW, so no council approval is needed if the standards are met. That changes if walls move, if the home is a heritage item or in one of City of Newcastle’s heritage conservation areas, or if it is in a strata building. In strata, a bathroom renovation is a major renovation that needs a special resolution.

Approvals depend on what you change and what kind of property you own. This guide summarises NSW and City of Newcastle rules, with links to the sources. It is not legal or planning advice.

Last updated: 26 November 2026

This page summarises official and industry information so you know what to expect. It is not legal or planning advice. Your renovator, a certifier, City of Newcastle or your strata manager can confirm what applies to your home.

Houses: when a bathroom renovation is exempt

In NSW, some minor work can be done without council approval as exempt development under the State Environmental Planning Policy (Exempt and Complying Development Codes) 2008, known as the Codes SEPP. City of Newcastle lists "minor building alterations (internal)" among the exempt development types, and points to the general requirements in the SEPP.

The Housing Industry Association explains that replacing or renovating a bathroom can be exempt under section 2.51(1)(c) of the Codes SEPP if the standards are met. It says that can include new waterproofing, re-tiling and new fixtures, and that fixtures such as the bath or vanity can be relocated. It stops being exempt if the work:

  • changes the configuration of a room, for example by removing or building a wall, even a non-loadbearing one. A complying development certificate or a development application is then needed
  • reduces the size of a window or door needed for light or ventilation
  • affects the load-bearing capacity of the building, or changes a fire-resisting component, such as by penetrating a wall or floor between apartments.

Sources: City of Newcastle, Exempt development; HIA, Are bathroom and kitchen renovations exempt development?

Heritage items and heritage conservation areas

City of Newcastle says that if a property is a heritage item or in a heritage conservation area (HCA), most building works need development consent. Heritage items are listed in Schedule 5 of the Newcastle Local Environmental Plan 2012. You can check a property on the NSW Planning Portal's ePlanning Spatial Viewer. Council lists nine HCAs:

  • Cameron's Hill
  • Cooks Hill
  • Glebe Road Federation Cottages
  • Hamilton Business Centre (Beaumont Street)
  • Hamilton Residential Precinct
  • Hamilton South "Garden Suburb"
  • Newcastle City Centre
  • Newcastle East
  • The Hill

For small jobs, Council has a Heritage Minor Works or Maintenance Notification. It is used to get written confirmation that development consent isn't needed, and Council says you must have that confirmation before starting work. Council lists "replacement or renovation of a non-original bathroom or kitchen" as work it will generally treat as minor. It doesn't treat changing a room's configuration, structural changes, or work that has already been done as minor. Council says applications typically take two weeks. If it declines, a development application is needed, and low-risk proposals may suit its Accelerated Development Application process. For State-listed properties, the application also needs Heritage NSW paperwork.

For contributory buildings in an HCA, Council's heritage FAQs say upgrades to kitchens, bathrooms and services to meet contemporary standards are generally acceptable.

Sources: City of Newcastle, Heritage minor works; City of Newcastle, Built heritage FAQs.

Apartments and other strata properties

Under NSW strata rules, a renovation that needs waterproofing is a major renovation, and the NSW Government gives bathroom renovations as an example. Major renovations:

  • can only be approved by the owners corporation, by special resolution at an AGM or other general meeting. That means no more than 25% of the value of votes cast are against it
  • need the resolution to say who is responsible for ongoing maintenance of the common property, the owners corporation or the lot owner
  • need 14 days' written notice to the owners corporation before work starts, if the work changes the structure of the property.

Without approval, you may have to pay to put the property back the way it was. Strata schemes must hold at least one meeting a year, so allow time to get the vote. Tenants should ask their landlord in writing, and the landlord then follows the strata approval process. Check your scheme's by-laws too.

Source: NSW Government, Strata renovation rules.

HIA also notes that the Design and Building Practitioners Act 2020 doesn't apply to most exempt bathroom renovations in a single dwelling. It can apply where interior waterproofing work relates to more than one dwelling, or where the work isn't exempt, so ask your renovator whether it applies in your building.

Licences and insurance still apply

Whatever the approval pathway, NSW licensing, contract and Home Building Compensation rules still apply, and waterproofing must be done by a licensed contractor. Our renovation process guide and waterproofing guide cover these.

Questions to ask a bathroom renovator

  • Will any walls, windows or doors change, and does that take the job out of exempt development?
  • Is my home a heritage item or in an HCA, and who will lodge the heritage minor works notification or DA?
  • For an apartment, what will you provide for the owners corporation's approval, such as plans, waterproofing details and licences?
  • Is time for approvals built into the start date in the quote?
